Kilometres · two astronomical units

Two viewpoints, six months apart. Earth in January and Earth in July are the widest-set pair of eyes we have.

Every distance beyond the solar system starts here. Photograph a star in January, photograph it again in July, and measure how far it moved against the far background. The nearer the star, the larger the shift. That is the whole method — the rest is arithmetic.

Sunlight crosses one astronomical unit in 8 minutes 19 seconds. You are always seeing the Sun as it was, never as it is.

Rung one · trigonometric parallax

0.768″

Proxima Centauri · 4.2465 light-years

The nearest star to the Sun shifts by about three quarters of an arcsecond — a one-pound coin seen from five kilometres away.

Divide one by that angle and the answer comes out in parsecs: 1.302, or 4.2465 light-years. The unit was built backwards from the method — a parsec is simply the distance at which a star shifts by one arcsecond.

Gaia has made this measurement for roughly 1.8 billion stars, the best of them to twenty millionths of an arcsecond. Past a few thousand light-years, though, the shift shrinks below what any instrument can trust, and the ladder needs a second rung.

Rung two · Leavitt's law

2,537,000 ly

Andromeda · measured by pulsing stars

In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt noticed that some stars pulse, and the slower they pulse the brighter they truly are.

That turns a clock into a ruler. Time the pulse and you know the star's real brightness; compare it with how bright it looks from here, and the shortfall is distance. Leavitt found the relation in 25 stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud, working from photographic plates.

Delta Cephei, the star the class is named for, brightens and dims on a 5.37-day cycle. It has been doing so, visibly, for as long as anyone has looked.

Rung three · standard candles

1,000,000,000

Light-years · Type Ia supernovae, and further still

Cepheids fade from view around a hundred million light-years. Exploding white dwarfs carry the ladder a thousand times further.

A white dwarf pulling matter from a companion detonates at a fixed threshold, so every Type Ia peaks at close to the same true brightness — bright enough to outshine its whole host galaxy for a few weeks. Same candle, different distances, measurable dimming.

In 1998 two competing teams pushed these measurements far enough out to find something nobody had ordered: the expansion is not slowing down. It is speeding up.

Rung four · redshift

13.8 billion years

Oldest light · horizon 46.5 billion ly

Past the candles, distance is read from colour. Expanding space stretches light on its way here, so the further it has travelled, the redder it arrives.

Each element absorbs at fixed, known wavelengths. Find that fingerprint in a distant galaxy's spectrum, measure how far it has slid toward red, and you have both its speed and its distance. The oldest light we can see set out 13.8 billion years ago; the space it crossed has stretched to 46.5 billion light-years since.

The two ways of measuring the expansion rate — this ladder, and the earliest light — still disagree by roughly eight percent. Nobody has resolved it. That gap is where the next physics is expected to be.
